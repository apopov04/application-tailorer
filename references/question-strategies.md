# Adapting by question purpose

Use this as writing guidance, not a claim about a hidden employer rubric.

| Question | Useful evidence | Adaptation trap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Why this company? | A supplied personal interest, a verified aspect of the organization, and a reason the connection matters | Recycling the old company’s mission or assuming the candidate has used the new product |
| Why this role? | Relevant work, what the candidate wants to do next, and the responsibilities in this posting | Writing only about the company’s reputation |
| Why you / what do you bring? | One or two demonstrated capabilities tied to the work | Repeating a skills list or overstating contribution |
| A challenge, failure or disagreement | The actual situation, candidate’s own action, result and learning | Turning a team action into a solo rescue; replacing a failure with a disguised success |
| What interests you about our product? | Verified product context plus a genuine observation or curiosity supplied by the candidate | Inventing product use, customer experience or expert knowledge |
| Values or judgment | A real decision and tradeoff, or the candidate’s own stated reasoning | Fabricating moral commitments to flatter the employer |
| Practical availability / eligibility | The candidate’s actual circumstances and the exact question | Giving persuasive prose where a precise answer is needed |

## Reuse without carrying over the wrong story

Treat a past answer as several components. Personal evidence can transfer; employer-specific claims often cannot. Voice can transfer even when the example does not. An old “why join” answer about learning from real users may help a new one, but a sentence about a particular defense mission does not become the candidate’s reason for joining an unrelated startup merely by changing the organization name.

A company-specific answer needs a bridge: relevant fact about this company → why that fact matters to this person → what they could contribute or learn in this role. If the middle is unknown, ask. Do not make up a motivation to complete the bridge.

## Match evidence to the prompt

For “what did you learn?”, spend space on the lesson and how it changed a later action. For “how did you handle disagreement?”, show the differing views and the candidate’s response; a project summary alone misses the point. For “describe impact”, explain what changed for someone, even if no metric exists. Do not imply that every employer expects a quantified business result from a junior candidate.

When a prompt asks two things, allocate room to both before drafting. Under a tight limit, one well-chosen example may support both. Cut background that the reader does not need to understand the answer.

## Batch coherence

Build a temporary map of question → main point → evidence → unique detail. This helps a set of responses feel consistent without sounding copied. A project can appear twice if one answer covers technical judgment and another a specific collaboration moment. Do not repeat its full introduction each time.

Check that a role is not called an internship in one answer and employment in another, a group outcome does not become individual ownership, and a motivation does not contradict another response. Surface real uncertainty rather than smoothing it over with impressive prose.

## Voice and finishing

Compare the rewrite with the user’s examples. Would this person plausibly say it? Remove inflated enthusiasm, unsupported superlatives and vocabulary absent from their writing unless it improves precision. Keep clear technical terms where relevant. A concise, concrete statement usually carries more information than “I am incredibly passionate about your innovative mission.”

Do not append analysis, citations or count labels inside text intended for a form. If the user requests a clean answer only, omit change notes unless an unresolved fact would otherwise mislead them.
